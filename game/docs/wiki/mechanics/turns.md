# Turns, driving and combat

The turn, orders, routes, crashes, guns, armor, utilities and the log. The principles behind them are in [DESIGN.md](../../DESIGN.md).

Movement and combat happen on the same map, in Space Rangers 2 style. Travel and fights use the same turns.

A truck is in combat while a hostile act of another truck against it, or of it against another truck, is recent. A hostile act is a shot, hit or miss, an emitter pulse, a claymore blast, a ram or crash between hostile trucks, or an NPC closing in on its target on a fight goal in sight. It starts combat for both trucks, and it ends 10 turns after the last act, or at once when the two stop being hostile, through a truce, mercy, a knockout or a tow rope. A hostile truck that only drives past, without shooting, ramming or hunting, is a warning and not combat. Combat blocks jobs like repairs and searches, looting, tow offers, radio topics other than the fight's and aid asks. The blocked search button shows the turns left. Planning does not advance time. Clicks set or change the order point and show the route preview. Outside combat, Space starts automatic turns until the truck reaches the point. Space pauses after the current turn without clearing the point, and another press resumes the route. Visible hostiles, combat, collisions, breakdowns, panels and loss of browser focus pause automatic travel. Without an order point, or in combat or direct-drive mode, Space advances one turn. Holding Space fast-forwards turns, including combat, until released. Loading a save starts paused. In combat or direct-drive mode, the player sets movement and weapon orders, then ends the turn. All vehicles move at the same time, then all weapons fire at the same time.

Each chassis has max speed, acceleration, braking and turn rates. Momentum carries over: the speed you have is a committed distance for next turn. Faster trucks turn less per turn. Vehicles are physics bodies with suspension. Time only runs while a turn plays: each turn simulates one second of driving. Slopes, bumps and collisions come from the physics. An engine pulls harder against a climb than on flat ground, so loaded trucks still crawl up steep hills, while starts on flat ground stay the same. A landing hurts by how hard the truck comes down into the ground, so driving over a crest and down the slope is safe, while a drop is not.

A click sets a point to drive through. A click on that point switches it to a stop, and Shift-click sets a stop at once. A stop brakes on the point and takes corners carefully. A click on a site stops at the site's pad nearest the truck. Clicking anywhere on your own moving truck brakes it, even where a rock covers it, and the pointer turns into a hand while it is on it.

The zones around a moving truck split its reach: a near click brakes, a middle click holds speed, and a far click accelerates. From rest, a nearby click starts the truck slowly. A click almost straight behind within reach backs toward that point. A farther click is a course: the truck turns around and follows a route around obstacles over as many turns as it takes, so Space alone carries it there. A point it cannot reach sends it to the closest point it can reach. After the order point, or with no order, the truck coasts on.

The truck plans a route around rocks, wrecks, parked vehicles and cliffs, preferring roads. Routes cross gentle hills rather than winding far around them, and take a straight line over bends when it costs little more. Every route planner weighs a road above its speed, since a road puts a driver where others can help. A far longer road detour still loses to open ground. Raiders that retreat, flee or are stranded are the one exception: they price roads far above open ground, so they drive beside a road and only cross it. The screen shows the planned path for the next turns.

Crash damage grows with the square of impact speed, and a slow bump does no damage. The lighter truck takes the bigger share, so heavy trucks win rams. A ram part on the striking side takes the blow and hits harder. Rams are the heaviest armor per cell, a fifth more than a steel plate, and stop fewer rounds than plates. So a truck carries a ram where it strikes, not as armor. A ram bar shields the truck behind it from a rock or a wall only below about 80 km/h. A harder crash breaks through to the parts behind. R toggles manual driving, which skips the route planner so the truck can ram. A truck that drives off a raised ramp edge, like the Fallen Sun's wing flaps, flies, and lands with wheel wear that grows with the square of its fall speed. Routes never plan a jump, so jumping takes manual driving. NPC drivers use the same steering and obstacle routing as the player. A stuck driver backs away before trying the route again.

Every gun's range fits inside sight, so a gun never outreaches its driver's eyes. Weapons have range, a magazine, a reload time, scatter, a firing arc and a number of rounds per shot. Scatter grows sharply toward the end of a gun's range, and cheap guns lose more aim there than good ones. A cheap gun at full range scatters four times as wide as up close, and a top gun twice as wide. A gun's arc is centered on the way the gun faces, which the player sets by turning the part. A 360 degree gun covers all sides. A narrower gun needs the truck to turn so the target lies inside its arc, and a machine gun's 270 degree arc leaves a blind spot behind its facing. A gun's barrel turns only where the gun can fire, so it shows which way a truck can shoot. Tall parts block fire: the cab, the big guns and box cargo frames. Seen from the gun's center, each tall cell hides the slice of angles between its corners, however far away it stands. The gun fires through its arc minus those slices. So a gun behind the cab cannot fire forward over it, a gun beside the cab loses only the angles the cab covers, and a cargo box behind a turret blinds its rear. Hovering or selecting a gun in the grid draws its firing fan and outlines the parts in its way. On the ground, the selected gun's reach shows. With no gun selected, no reach shows.

Heavy guns kick. Recoil adds scatter that falls with truck mass, so a tank gun sprays on a scout and holds steady on a tractor. Each gun also has its own shake from the truck's speed. A stabilized MG fires well on the move, and a sniper cannon needs a parked truck. Each round rolls on its own. It hits when its scatter is smaller than the target's width as seen from the gun. So distance, target size, facing, crossing speed, the shooter's own speed and round speed all matter. Hovering a truck shows both sides' chances and their causes.

Each gun fires from a magazine. When the magazine runs dry, the gun reloads for a few turns, and a gun that holds fire long enough refills a half-empty magazine. The player can drop a magazine to reload early. An empty gun clunks, so the player hears when an enemy MG goes quiet and it is safe to close in for a strike up close. The weapon panel shows the player's ammo, and the hover card shows the ammo of the hovered truck's guns.

Guns come in three hidden roles. Damagers, like shotguns, cannons and rockets, wreck the parts behind armor but barely dent the armor itself. They have narrow arcs and wide scatter. Chippers, like the machine guns, strip armor fast but do little to the parts behind it. Precision guns reach farthest, out to the edge of sight, and keep their aim on the move. They land fewer rounds and less damage, and armor soaks them, so their strength is hitting without being hit. Each tier has one gun per role and one gun per pair of roles. So a fight has a plan: chip the plates off, then finish with a cannon, or pick off a part from afar. Duels in the combat harness hold these goals. A damager beats a chipper or a precision gun on bare trucks about 70% of the time. A chipper beats either on armored trucks about as often. A precision gun swings more with the situation than any other matchup. A gun a tier up beats its own role a tier down at least two times in three.

A round that misses its target can hit another truck near the line of fire. Some guns, like shotguns and rockets, stray much more than others. A round with splash explodes where it lands, hit or miss, and hurts every truck in reach. A cannon shell, rocket or grenade that bursts on open ground leaves a crater. Its rim jolts a truck that drives over it, and the crater fades after a couple of days, once nobody watches. Water, canals and hull plating take no craters. A driver hit by accident knows who the shooter aimed at and lets it go, unless the accidental damage adds up to a lot. Then it starts a feud with the shooter. A truck of the shooter's own faction, or one with a deal with it like a hired escort, always lets it go.

There is no hull. A round enters from the side facing the shooter and walks the grid cell by cell. Each working part it meets takes damage, and the part's armor uses up the round's penetration. Rounds are kinetic or blast. Rockets, cannon shells and all splash are blast. Armor has a separate number against blast: a rebar cage stops rockets but lets bullets through, and ceramic stops bullets but cracks under blast. An aimed shot targets one part's lane, and a near miss still hits where it lands. The hit chance the player sees is the chance a round damages the aimed part, or any part for a body shot. It counts parts in the way, lost penetration and splash, so a part shielded from this side shows a low chance and the hover card names parts in the way. A part at 0 HP stops working. A dead weapon cannot fire. A dead engine or transmission limits speed to a crawl. Dead wheels cut speed and steering, and a holed tank leaks fuel. A destroyed cab ends the fight: the truck is knocked out. So may a cab below half: each turn of cab damage there may knock the driver out first, more likely for more damage and a weaker cab, and about half of the drivers still fight on until the cab breaks. An aim at a part that leaves its truck, by a refit, a loot or a sale, becomes a body shot at the same truck.

Q toggles weapon auto mode. It gives every weapon a body shot at the nearest hostile it can hit. Utilities never join it. Clicking a target switches it off. NPC weapons target their chosen opponent or a visible attacker that shot at them or a nearby faction mate. Retreat does not disable defensive fire. NPCs rarely start attacks near a lawman town's gate, but nothing stops them firing there. A robber is no defender: once it drops the robbery, its victim shooting back does not free its guns.

Beating a truck pays nothing by itself. The loot is on the truck. A wreck leaves parts worth a share of its chassis value, scaled by its HP left, so a kill pays well but not more than trading. Camps refill their raiders over about a day.

The log is for important gameplay and role-play events. It gives the player extra information and details they need. It never lists mechanical state, like gun cooldowns, magazines or utility reloads, which the HUD shows. Damage readouts are one of the few exceptions: each shot logs the parts it damaged, so the player can study their combat tactics.

When several things are in reach, such as a shop and a loot pile or two knocked-out trucks, the left and right arrow keys choose which one [E] uses, and the action button shows the choice with its place in the list.

## Utilities

Utilities act on an order. An order is for the current turn and clears once the turn plays. The weapon panel has a utility row of up to four slots: the truck's active utilities and claymore rams. The number keys run the panel in order: the guns take 1 and up, and the utility slots take the next numbers after the last gun, up to 9. So no two slots share a key, and a slot past 9 is used by click. Each slot has one state, with its own look beyond its text:

- Ready: a lit mustard frame.
- Aiming: the selected point utility, filled bright mustard with a crosshair badge and a pulsing outline. At most one slot aims.
- Set: an order that fires this turn, with a green frame and a check badge.
- Armed: a claymore ram ready to blow, with a red frame and a fuse badge.
- Recharging: a grayed icon, its turns left and a bar that fills as the reload counts down.
- Blocked: broken, shut down, out of fuel or off the deck, with a dark red hatched frame and the reason.

A press, by key or click, works by the kind of utility:

- On the Sprout, Caltrops, Oil spiller, Emitter or a claymore ram, it sets "use this turn".
- On the Smoke mortar or the Flare cannon, it shows the reach band on the ground and the effect's outline under the pointer, and waits for a ground click inside the band. That click sets the point instead of a move order, and a small cross marks it. No sight of the point is needed.

The same key or Escape drops a waiting selection, and a press on a slot with an order clears it. A broken, unmounted, shut-down or recharging part takes no order. Aim marks show only while planning and hide while a turn plays. NPCs choose their orders by their own rules, and the orders pass the same checks and act the same way.

All uses resolve after the trucks move and before the guns fire. Smoke and flares come first, so a Sprout used this turn already covers this turn's shots. Then the ground drops land and claymores arm, and the emitter pulses last.

Smoke spoils aim. The Sprout puts a cloud of 5 tiles around the truck for 6 turns. The Smoke mortar puts a cloud of 4 tiles on a point 5 to 16 tiles away for 5 turns. A cloud sits still, so where it looks thick is where it counts. It is drawn as a dense black plume that hides the ground at its middle, with no ring at its edge. Puffs over the player's own truck thin out, so the player always finds it. A shot whose line from shooter to target touches any cloud gets the smoke cause, about 6 degrees of extra scatter. That holds with the shooter inside, the target inside, both inside or the line crossing a cloud, and several clouds count once. Like every cause, it shrinks against a parked target. Smoke blocks no sight and no line of fire, so it is not cover. Every truck's guns feel it, the harpoon too, and the hover card names it. Town and camp guns roll their own way and ignore it.

Caltrops and the Oil spiller drop on the ground the truck drove over this turn, behind it in its travel, so a reversing truck drops ahead of its nose, on the ground it backed over. Past where the turn's drive began, the line goes on straight the way the drive started. A truck that moved less than a quarter tile drops behind its rear. The drop's near edge lies 1 tile behind the rear, so the dropper starts clear of it. Each drop appears as the truck passes it while the turn plays.

Caltrops drop a field of 1.25 tiles. A caltrop field lasts 10 turns. A truck whose path this turn comes within the field, counting its own size, takes 8 damage on each of its four wheels, once per field. That holds for the dropper too, and standing on the field costs nothing more. The damage counts like stray fire from the dropper: an attack on a truck already hostile to it, otherwise an accident that can add up to a feud. The Oil spiller spends 2 fuel from the tank, and it cannot be used with less. It lays a slick of 6 overlapping oil patches of 0.9 tiles, 0.8 tiles apart, along the path: a streak about 5.8 tiles long that follows the drive, bends included. The slick lasts 8 turns. A wheel on oil keeps 12% of its grip, and overlapping patches count once. A truck whose rear wheel first reaches oil in a turn above 4 tiles per turn gets a tail kick: a turn of 1.2 rad/s for each 4 tiles per turn above that, at most 1.6 rad/s. It swings the tail toward the oiled rear wheel, or with the truck's turn when both rear wheels reach oil together, and once per truck per turn. The low grip lets the swing grow into a slide, so a truck crossing a slick fast loses its line. At or below 4 tiles per turn there is no kick, and a straight crossing keeps its line. There is no random skid. The route preview runs the same physics, so it shows the slide. A slide is not a crash by itself. Route planners, the player's and the NPCs', steer around the fields their driver sees now or dropped itself, as around a parked truck. Manual driving can still go in.

### Harpoon

The Harpoon is a gun, not a utility. It sits in the weapon panel on a gun key, and it aims, waits, holds fire and auto fires like any gun. It reaches 8 tiles in a 270 degree arc and fires one barbed bolt, rolled like any round, so every scatter cause applies, smoke too. Its reach is short, so it aims tight and leads a moving truck well: from 5 tiles it lands about 95% on a parked truck and 65% on one crossing at 4 tiles a turn. Its magazine holds one bolt. After a shot, hit or miss, it reloads for 5 turns and fires again in the sixth, and its slot shows the reload until then.

The bolt flies as fast as a cannon shell, with the other guns, and trails its rope from the launcher. The launcher turns toward its target like any turret. The rope is thin and black, and it hangs: it sags when slack, runs straight when stretched and rests on the ground. A bolt that lands on the target ties the line for 10 turns from the turn it fires. While its line is out, the harpoon holds fire, reloaded or not, and its slot reads "line out". It holds the first working part in the lane the bolt struck, even one past what the bolt can pierce. A lane with no working part, as an outer lane of empty armor slots, passes the hold to the nearest lane that has one. A bolt that misses falls past the target, its rope lies on the ground, and then it reels back into the launcher. The log names the part the line holds, or that the bolt missed.

The line's length is the distance between its anchors at the hit. While the trucks are farther apart than that, it pulls both together, and it never pushes. The rope is stiff, so it stretches under a meter in most drags. Any truck can drag the truck it hooked, or be dragged by it, for all 10 turns, from rest or at speed, whether the other truck brakes or flees. Only the stretch counts toward a tear, not the jerk of the line going taut. A tear takes a pull past anything a drag between two trucks reaches, and it damages the part the line held by 12. The harpoon is braced to its truck's frame, so its own line never tips the shooter. The line pulls the held truck high on its body, so a hard pull across a truck of like weight tips it over. A pull along a truck's length does not tip it. A heavier truck drags the shooter along but is not tipped. The player cuts the line with the scissors button in the harpoon's slot, in place of reload. A cut does no damage, and the harpoon fires again once reloaded. The line also ends when either anchor part leaves its truck or the harpoon breaks. When a line ends, its rope reels back into the launcher. It passes through props, and the two trucks crash into each other like any trucks.

The Emitter pulses over 6 tiles around the truck. It hits every other truck there, allies and neutrals too, and spares its user. Each truck hit takes the pulse as a shot at it. The pulse changes nothing in its own turn, so no shot fired with it is lost. The trucks it hit are shut down for the next two turns. A shut-down engine gives no drive force, but steering and brakes work, and the truck rolls on with its momentum, the slope and friction. Its guns and utilities cannot fire, its scanner is off, and its engine makes no sound and does not heat. An armed claymore stays armed and a harpoon line holds. The hover card shows a shut-down truck's turns left.

An armed claymore ram blasts on the next crash on its own side with another truck or an obstacle, at a closing speed of at least 3 tiles per turn. The other truck takes a blast of 60 damage and 12 penetration, 2 m around the middle of the side the crash touched. The user's struck lanes take 25 damage and 6 penetration, so the ram soaks first. Against an obstacle only the user's lanes take the blast, and the obstacle stands. The blast throws the two trucks apart, or the user back off the obstacle, with a push that lifts them a little. The blast is the owner's attack and counts for the kill. The crash damage applies as usual. A ram the crash breaks still blows. Slower crashes, crashes on another side, crashes with the ground, a rail or the map edge, and tows leave it armed. Arming is not hostile. An armed claymore that breaks on its mount, under gunfire or in a crash that did not set it off, cooks off after the guns fire: the full blast enters its own truck through the ram's lanes, and the reload starts. A claymore that moves or leaves its mount loses its charge.
